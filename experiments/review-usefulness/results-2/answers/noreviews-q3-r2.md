## Short answer

Not for the open question "is this response better for this user?". An LLM judge is a usable stand-in only when it is handed the user's criteria and merely checks them, and even then the "humans" it agrees with are third-party annotators, not the user.

## Where it fails: judging personalisation quality directly

The one paper in the library that tests this head-on is *Re-Centering Humans in LLM Personalization* (2026). Raters saw a user's attributes, a prompt, and a generic and a personalised response side by side.

- **Judges inflate.** Humans average 3.18 on a 1–5 preference scale (3 = no better than generic); the five LLM judges average 3.41–4.02 ([p. 15, Table 6](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/methods/Re-Centering%20Humans%20in%20LLM%20Personalization.pdf?page=15&search=Agreement%20between%20LLM%20judges)).
- **Correlation is weak.** Spearman with the human mean is 0.11–0.38. Trained reward models reach only about 0.3 ([p. 9, §6.3](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/methods/Re-Centering%20Humans%20in%20LLM%20Personalization.pdf?dest=subsection.6.3&page=9)).
- **The bias has a mechanism.** Several open-weight judges reward explicit mentions like "Given your interest in machine learning…", which humans do not. Models that write such mentions more often also reward them more as judges (Spearman 0.90, on five models, p = 0.04) ([p. 8, §6.2](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/methods/Re-Centering%20Humans%20in%20LLM%20Personalization.pdf?dest=subsection.6.2&page=8)).
- **The same happens upstream.** On which attributes are relevant to a prompt, humans say yes about 20% of the time and LLMs 40–60%. LLM–human κ is 0.30, against 0.43 human–human and 0.60 LLM–LLM ([p. 6, §5.2](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/methods/Re-Centering%20Humans%20in%20LLM%20Personalization.pdf?dest=subsection.5.2&page=6)).

Two caveats on this evidence:

- **Small sample:** the response-quality stage is 80 items and 367 ratings.
- **Low human ceiling:** human–human Spearman is only 0.325 (p. 8), so a judge at 0.36–0.38 is already about as close to the human mean as one annotator is to another. The low correlation is therefore weak evidence; the inflation and the mention bias are the solid findings.

## Where it works: checking stated criteria

Agreement with humans rises as the judge is given more of what the user wants.

| Paper | What the judge is given | Agreement with humans |
|---|---|---|
| CUPID | A stated preference to match against | Krippendorff α 0.769 on 230 items ([p. 3](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/benchmarks/CUPID.%20Evaluating%20Personalized%20and%20Contextualized%20Alignment%20of%20LLMs%20from%20Interactions.pdf?page=3&search=Krippendorff)) |
| LaMP-QA | Rubric aspects extracted from the asker's own question narrative | 73% pairwise agreement, against 58% for scoring without aspects ([p. 7](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/benchmarks/LaMP-QA.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Personalized%20Long-form%20Question%20Answering.pdf?page=7&search=aspect-based%20evaluation%20method%20selects)) |
| Personalized RewardBench | The true rubric aspects (oracle) | 97.8–99.1% accuracy, against at most 75.9% without them (p. 15, p. 6) |
| PersonaLens | Profile plus guidelines | κ 0.52 on personalisation, against 0.78 on task completion ([p. 8, Table 5](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/benchmarks/PersonaLens.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Personalization%20Evaluation%20in%20Conversational%20AI%20Assistants.pdf?dest=table.5&page=8)) |
| OP-Bench | An over-personalisation rubric | ρ ≈ 0.68 for the judge used, 0.74 for the best one (p. 13–14) |
| AlpsBench | Gold memories and task-specific criteria | 73–96% alignment by task ([p. 4](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/benchmarks/AlpsBench.%20An%20LLM%20Personalization%20Benchmark%20for%20Real-Dialogue%20Memorization%20and%20Preference%20Alignment.pdf?page=4&search=LLM-as-a-Judge%20Alignment)) |

Things to keep in mind when reading this table:

- **Personalisation is the weakest dimension.** In PersonaLens the same judge does clearly worse on personalisation than on task completion.
- **The RewardBench oracle is partly by construction.** The rejected responses were generated by telling a model to avoid the rubric, so a judge holding the rubric has an easy job.
- **Format and judge size matter.** In LaMP-QA a pairwise Qwen2.5-32B judge flipped its choice in 78% of cases when the order was reversed, and a 0.5B judge agreed with humans 48% of the time with a mean score of 0.94, against 73% and 0.44 for the 32B judge (p. 7).

## The gap none of these close

Every "human" above is an outside annotator guessing what a user would want. LaMP-QA says outright that the most reliable evaluator is the original user (p. 10).

The only first-party evidence here is PRISM-X, which re-recruited 530 real participants and also simulated each one with GPT-4o given their profile ([p. 16–17](http://pdf.invalid/llm/personalization/benchmarks/PRISM-X.%20Experiments%20on%20Personalised%20Fine-Tuning%20with%20Human%20and%20Simulated%20Users.pdf?page=16)):

- **Aggregate level:** simulated and real rankings of the four models agree almost perfectly (r = 0.98–0.99).
- **Individual level:** per-trial Kendall τ is 0.11–0.22, against a human self-consistency of 0.57. Top-1 agreement is 32–40%, against 68.8%.
- **Bias:** simulators pick position A as best 44.9% of the time and position D 7.2% (p. 18).

So a persona-conditioned judge can tell you which system wins on average, but not whether a given response suited a given person, which is the thing personalisation is meant to deliver.

## What I would do in practice

- **Turn the judgment into a check.** Have the judge verify explicit, user-derived criteria (LaMP-QA aspects, CUPID preference matching) rather than rate "how personalised" a response is.
- **Keep a human-labelled slice and correct with it.** Prediction-powered inference uses a small human set to remove the judge's measured bias from the estimate; this targets exactly the inflation above. The paper has no LLM-judge experiment, so that use is by analogy.
- **Let the judge abstain.** *Trust or Escalate* calibrates a confidence threshold so that agreement with humans is guaranteed on the items the judge does score; the abstained items turn out to be the subjective ones (p. 7). For personalisation, expect low coverage.
- **Use real users for the final claim.** Anything of the form "users prefer the personalised system" needs first-party ratings, as in PRISM-X.
