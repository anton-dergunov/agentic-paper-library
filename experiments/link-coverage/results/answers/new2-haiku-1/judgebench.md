**Short version:** read pp. 4–6 for the construction, then pp. 19 for the filtering details. Everything else is results or supporting material.

**How it's built, in four steps:**

1. **Start from a dataset with a checker.** Take any dataset that has ground-truth answers and an automatic verifier. JudgeBench uses MMLU-Pro (knowledge), LiveBench reasoning and math, and LiveCodeBench (coding). [p. 5](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=5)
2. **Sample and grade.** Sample k responses per question from one strong model (GPT-4o), grade each one, and keep only questions that have at least one correct and one incorrect response. [p. 5](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=5); Figure 2 is on [p. 4](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=4).
3. **Pair them.** Each pair is one correct and one incorrect response from the same model. The core argument: a model that can't reliably produce correct answers to a hard question also can't reliably tell its correct answers from its wrong ones, so the pairs are hard for judges. Same-model pairs also remove style differences and self-enhancement bias. [p. 5](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=5)
4. **Clean the labels.** Automatic regex checks reject correct answers over formatting, so GPT-4o-mini re-grades each answer and any disagreement is dropped. [pp. 5–6](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=6); the details are in [A.5, p. 19](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=19).

The final GPT-4o split is 350 pairs: 154 knowledge, 98 reasoning, 56 math, 42 coding. Sampling and filtering are in [A.3, p. 19](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=19).

**Must-read sections, in order:**

- **§3, pp. 4–5:** the pipeline and why it's built this way. This is the core.
- **§4 opening and the aggregation rule, p. 6:** each pair is judged twice with the order swapped, and ties and inconsistent verdicts count as wrong. You need this to read any number in the paper.
- **A.3 and A.5, p. 19:** how many questions were sampled, and how the labels were checked.
- **A.4, p. 19:** length control. Correct and incorrect responses average about 562 vs 561 tokens, so length can't explain the results.
- **§4.4 and Figure 4, pp. 9–10:** the generator-bias check. Judges score much lower on pairs generated by Claude-3.5-Sonnet than on GPT-4o pairs (Claude goes from 64.3% to 44.8%), so GPT-4o-generated pairs are biased against GPT-4o judges.

**Useful for results:** Table 1 and Table 2 on [p. 7](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=7), and the reward-model table and takeaways on [p. 8](http://pdf.invalid/llm/evaluation/methods/JudgeBench.%20A%20Benchmark%20for%20Evaluating%20LLM-based%20Judges.pdf?page=8). The headline is that GPT-4o is near chance (50.9% overall with the vanilla prompt), and the best judge scores are around 80% (o3-mini, high).

**Safe to skip:** the related work on pp. 2–3, the judge descriptions in A.1, the fine-tuned-judge analysis in A.2, and the prompts in A.6. Read A.6 only if you want to reproduce the judges.

**Caveats to keep in mind:**

- The main split comes from a single generator (GPT-4o), so its results carry the generator bias described above.
- The paper's own augmented-set check (Table 8, p. 20) shows that the rankings hold, but the overall pair count is still small (350).
