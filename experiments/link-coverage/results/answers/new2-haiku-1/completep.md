**Main idea:** Depth-scaling rules for transformers can make learning rates transfer across depth, but some of them still leave the deeper layers "lazy." The authors propose CompleteP, a parameterisation that does both, and it trains compute-cheaper deep models.

**The mechanism.** Each residual block's output is multiplied by a depth-dependent factor before it is added to the residual stream, $h_{\ell+1} = h_\ell + L^{-\alpha} F_\ell(h_\ell)$, with $\alpha \in [0.5, 1]$ (p. 2, Eq. 1). Earlier work disagreed about the right $\alpha$: Yang et al. argued for $\alpha = 0.5$ and said hyperparameter transfer across depth is impossible at any $\alpha$, while Bordelon et al. favoured $\alpha = 1$ ([p. 2, Intro](http://pdf.invalid/deep-learning/optimizers-and-schedules/Don't%20be%20lazy.%20CompleteP%20enables%20compute-efficient%20deep%20transformers.pdf?page=2)).

**The claims.**
- **Transfer across depth.** Only $\alpha = 1$ lets the optimal learning rate and initialisation scale carry over from shallow to deep models (2 to 128 layers). The authors call this a result the earlier theory said was impossible.
- **Non-lazy learning.** Transfer alone is not enough. With some parameterisations, layers learn only features close to their linearisation, so depth and nonlinearity go unused. CompleteP is defined as the unique $\alpha$ that keeps every layer learning features, which is why it is named that way ([p. 1, Abstract](http://pdf.invalid/deep-learning/optimizers-and-schedules/Don't%20be%20lazy.%20CompleteP%20enables%20compute-efficient%20deep%20transformers.pdf?page=1)).
- **Modern training.** For pre-LayerNorm transformers trained with AdamW, the authors also rescale the LayerNorm learning rate, bias learning rate, weight decay and Adam's ε with depth and width. They report these changes are needed for stable training at $\alpha = 0.5$ (p. 2).
- **Compute.** At 1.9B parameters, CompleteP saves 11.8% of FLOPs over µP for compute-optimally shaped models and 34.4% for a 179-layer model. Across the paper it reports 12–34% efficiency gains and a wider range of width-to-depth ratios that stay compute-efficient (p. 1; p. 2).

This is a small-scale empirical result on Cerebras hardware, and the paper's own results are the evidence for it. Its theory section (Section 6) is what supports the "non-lazy" argument; I have not read that part yet. The paper's related work (p. 3) places it against the other depth-scaling proposals.
